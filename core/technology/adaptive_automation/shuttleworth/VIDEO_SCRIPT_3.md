# Video 3: What Does Open Mean to You?

**Target length:** 2–3 minutes
**Tone:** Conviction without preaching. This is the philosophical core.

---

## Script

Open means the grammar of life is not owned.

Let me explain what I mean by that.

We are entering a period where it becomes possible to design living systems —
organisms engineered for specific functions, grown rather than manufactured,
biological rather than industrial. This is going to happen. The protein language
models exist. The regulatory prediction models exist. The gene editing tools exist.
The question is not whether. The question is who controls the design layer.

Right now, the trajectory is clear. A small number of well-funded companies —
Ginkgo Bioworks, Zymergen before it collapsed, the defense-adjacent synthetic
biology firms — are building proprietary platforms for biological design. The
tools they build work. But they are closed. The codon tables, the compatibility
engines, the pathway databases, the design interfaces — all proprietary. If you
want to design a living material, you go through them or you don't do it at all.

This is the Microsoft model applied to biology. One company owns the operating
system. Everyone else is a user.

I think that's the wrong model for the language of life.

Here's why.

The organisms we're designing with — fungi, bacteria, plants — they evolved
over billions of years through an open process. Evolution doesn't have IP.
The genetic code is shared. The regulatory logic is shared. The developmental
programs are variations on shared themes. The knowledge we're encoding into
these tools was generated by life itself, studied by publicly funded researchers,
published in open-access journals, stored in public databases like NCBI and
UniProt and the UCSC Genome Browser.

To take that commons — that shared inheritance — and lock it behind a proprietary
design interface is an act of enclosure. It's the same move that's been made with
land, with software, with data. And it produces the same result: concentration of
power, exclusion of the people who could do the most interesting work with it,
and designs that serve the interests of whoever owns the platform.

What I'm building is the open alternative.

The compatibility engine — codon optimization, regulatory element analysis,
pathway conflict detection — will be open source. The gene regulatory network
database will be open. The core bridge between English and biological specification
will be open. Anyone can use it. Any iGEM team, any small lab, any architect
experimenting with mycelium, any researcher in a country that can't afford
Ginkgo's platform fees.

What we monetize is the hosted platform, the advanced simulation, the enterprise
features, the synthesis-ready export. The Red Hat model. The Ubuntu model. The
infrastructure is commons. The product is convenience, speed, and support.

This matters because I believe the most important designs won't come from
corporate labs. They'll come from a graduate student in Nairobi who has access
to the same design tools as a team at MIT. They'll come from an architect in
rural Japan who wants to grow a tea house from local mycelium. They'll come
from an iGEM team that has an idea nobody at Ginkgo would fund because it
doesn't fit their commercial pipeline.

Open means those people get to play. Open means the living age doesn't belong
to whoever gets there first with the most capital. Open means the grammar of
life stays where it started — in the commons, where it evolved.

That's what open means to me.

## Delivery Notes

- "Open means the grammar of life is not owned" — this is the thesis sentence.
  Say it once at the top and once at the bottom. Let it bookend the whole video.
- The Microsoft/biology parallel should be stated plainly, not angrily —
  it's an observation, not an accusation
- The examples at the end (Nairobi, Japan, iGEM) should feel specific and real,
  not hypothetical virtue signaling — these are actual users of the system
- The Red Hat / Ubuntu model reference connects directly to Shuttleworth's own
  history — this will land with the reviewers
- End quiet and certain. Not passionate. Certain. The difference matters.
