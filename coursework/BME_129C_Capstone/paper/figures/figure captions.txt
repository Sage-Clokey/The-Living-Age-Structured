Layer 1: Network Topology

    layer1_topology.png (3 panels)
    1. Degree distribution — shows the E. coli GRN follows a heavy-tailed
    (scale-free-like) distribution, not a bell curve. No master node controls the
    network; influence is distributed with a few coordinating hubs, like a market with
    large firms but no central planner.
    2. Robustness curves — plots the fraction of the network that stays connected as
    you remove nodes (random vs targeted attack, Albert et al. 2000 method). Biological
     networks survive random failure but are vulnerable to targeted hub removal — same
    as a free market vs a planned economy losing its central bureau.
    3. Comparison bars — betweenness centrality, Gini coefficient, clustering across
    bio networks vs reference architectures (star, random, lattice, hub-spoke, BA).
    Shows biology sits between pure hierarchy and pure randomness — spontaneous order,
    not design.

    Layer 1 Self-Regulation (WBPA)

    layer1_self_regulation.png (3 panels)
    1. Degree vs betweenness scatter — for each network, plots node degree against
    betweenness centrality with Spearman rho annotated. In biological networks, high
    degree does NOT guarantee high betweenness — hubs don't monopolize information
    flow. This is the invisible hand in topology.
    2. Spearman correlation bars — bio networks vs reference architectures. Bio
    networks show lower degree-betweenness correlation than star or hub-spoke, proving
    they self-regulate against centralization (Topirceanu's WBPA mechanism).
    3. Gini ratio bars — betweenness inequality across networks. Biology is more
    egalitarian than engineered hierarchies.

    layer1_hub_erosion.png (2 panels)
    1. Per-hub betweenness change — when you artificially connect more edges to a top
    hub, does its betweenness go up (centralization) or down (self-regulation)? In bio
    networks, betweenness drops — the network resists monopoly. In star networks, it
    increases.
    2. Erosion rate comparison — BA (degree-PA) vs WBPA vs random growth models.
    WBPA-grown networks show hub erosion like biology; BA networks don't. This is the
    mechanistic test: biology grows by betweenness-preferential attachment, which
    automatically punishes hubs that get too powerful.

    layer1b_cell_economy.png (multi-panel)
    - UMAP of pbmc3k cell types, Shannon entropy (specialization), communication

    layer1b_directed_communication.png
    - Directed graph of ligand-receptor signaling between cell types, colored by
    pathway. Arrows show voluntary exchange: A produces a ligand, B acts on it. Both
    benefit. No cell is forced to participate — Rothbardian voluntary exchange at the
    molecular level.

    layer1b_comparative_advantage.png
    - Heatmap: rows = cell types, columns = enriched genes. Each cell type specializes
    in producing specific gene products at far higher levels than others — Mengerian
    comparative advantage. T cells don't try to make what monocytes make.

    layer1b_robustness_comparison.png
    - Grouped bars: distributed (green) vs star/centralized (red) communication
    networks. Shows what fraction of communication edges survive when you remove each
    cell type. The distributed biological network is far more robust — Rothbard's
    argument that centralization creates fragility.

    layer1b_price_signals.png
    - Radial diagrams showing how the same ligand is received differently by different
    cell types. This IS Menger's subjective value: the same molecule has different
    "prices" (biological effects) depending on who receives it. Value is not intrinsic
    to the signal.

    Layer 2: Economic Modeling

    layer2_economy.png (3 panels)
    1. GDP over time — distributed (green) vs centralized (red) metabolic allocation.
    Both converge, but distributed reaches equilibrium through price discovery (local
    feedback), centralized through top-down assignment.
    2. Perturbation robustness — GDP fraction retained after removing each pathway
    agent. Distributed retains more GDP across all knockouts — the market adapts, the
    plan breaks.
    3. Production rate convergence — individual agent production rates over time in the
     distributed regime. They start chaotic and converge to stable rates WITHOUT any
    coordinator — this is spontaneous order, the invisible hand finding equilibrium.

    layer2_perturbation_suite.png (2×2 panels)
    1. Substrate shock (Hayek) — cut supply of a key substrate by 80% at step 100. GDP
    curves for distributed vs centralized (dumb) vs centralized (smart). Distributed
    recovers faster because each agent independently discovers the new equilibrium. The
     smart planner has perfect information but still re-optimizes backward-looking.
    2. ATP crisis (Mises) — halve ATP regeneration for 30 steps. Measures trough depth.
     The calculation problem: even with full metabolite data, the planner can't
    anticipate which agents should sacrifice first.
    3. Demand spike (Kirzner) — 3x demand for one product. Measures response time.
    Distributed agents "discover" the opportunity through local price signals (product
    level drops → increase production). The planner waits for the next optimization
    cycle.
    4. Novel opportunity (Kirzner) — introduce a brand new resource. Measures GDP gain.
     Distributed agents that can use it start immediately; the planner has no
    pre-existing allocation rule for something it's never seen.

    Layer 2 FBA (just built)

    layer2_fba_analysis.png (3 panels)
    1. FBA vs Keio knockout scatter — each dot is a gene. X-axis = actual growth after
    knockout (Keio collection data). Y-axis = FBA-predicted growth. Green dots =
    correct predictions. Red dots = planner failures. The omniscient planner (FBA)
    achieves high but NOT 100% accuracy — Mises was right, perfect calculation is
    impossible.
    2. Confusion matrix — TP/TN/FP/FN. False positives = planner says essential but
    cell reroutes (Kirznerian adaptation). False negatives = planner says viable but
    cell dies (Hayekian local knowledge the LP can't encode). Each error type maps to a
     specific Austrian failure mode.
    3. Shadow prices — the dual variables from the LP. These ARE Hayekian price signals
     computed by the planner. They show the marginal value of each metabolite. The
    irony: FBA must compute these prices to solve the optimization, proving Hayek's
    point that prices contain essential information.

    layer2_fba_perturbation.png (3 panels)
    1. Glucose → Acetate (Hayek) — FBA instantly re-optimizes; real E. coli shows a
    diauxic lag. The planner assumes instant knowledge; biology must discover the new
    environment through distributed sensing.
    2. Aerobic → Anaerobic (Mises) — FBA re-solves; real E. coli uses ArcAB/FNR
    regulatory cascades. The planner computes the answer; biology grows toward it
    through local feedback.
    3. Nitrogen limitation (Kirzner) — FBA predicts the new optimum; real E. coli
    upregulates high-affinity transporters via NtrBC. Entrepreneurial alertness at the
    molecular level.

    Layer 3: Cross-Species Trade

    layer3_trade_network.png
    - Network graph of 5 organisms (E. coli, yeast, human, C. elegans, Drosophila)
    connected by trade links weighted by codon usage distance + regulatory barrier.
    Shows the cost of "trade" (heterologous expression) between species — Ricardian
    comparative advantage applied to biology.

    layer3_voluntary_exchange.png (3 panels)
    1. Trade cost vs success scatter — each dot is a real published gene transfer (GFP,
     insulin, silk, etc.). Lower trade cost = higher success. Voluntary exchange
    (compatible organisms) works; forced exchange (high barriers) fails. Rothbard:
    coercion destroys value.
    2. Success by compatibility tier — grouped bars. High-compatibility pairs (E.
    coli↔yeast) succeed more than low-compatibility pairs (bacteria↔human). The
    market self-selects for viable trades.
    3. Information destruction — shows that forcing a gene into an incompatible host
    destroys biological information (misfolding, wrong codon usage, missing
    chaperones). This is Mises' calculation problem applied to synthetic biology: you
    can't centrally plan protein folding.

    layer3_trade_blocs.png
    - Network with convex hulls around Louvain-detected communities. Organisms cluster
    into "trade blocs" based on phylogenetic compatibility — NOT because anyone planned
     it, but because compatible partners naturally trade more. Mengerian spontaneous
    order: institutions (trade relationships) emerge from individual action, not
    design.

    Layer 2 Price System: "The Price System of the Cell"

    price_system_of_the_cell.png (4 panels)

    The central figure of the paper. If prices are ratios of exchange that carry
    distributed information about scarcity and opportunity cost, then living
    systems have a real price system — and cancer is specifically its destruction.

    What if the disease isn't the variants — what if it's the system that stopped
    reading them? Is an elephant diseased because it can't climb a tree? Variants
    are not diseases. The disease is when you don't help maintain what exists.
    Anything can be a disease in the wrong environment. It is better to study
    variants for what will help them thrive than to decide what is a disease and
    what is not. The differences are a feature, not a bug — they are how
    adaptation is possible. It is trial and error directed by distributed
    knowledge. Eugenics is the same fatal conceit: you can't centrally plan life.

    Disease is the central planning, not the variation. Disease is the thorns
    stepping above instead of within. Disease is the state.

    Panel A: "The Price System of the Cell" — Conceptual diagram showing three
    tiers of cellular prices:
        Tier 1 (Intracellular): ATP/ADP ratio (energy budget), NAD+/NADH
        (redox state), AMP/ATP (scarcity alarm). These are the "cost of
        capital" — ratios that emerge from the cell's own metabolic activity,
        not set by any central authority. Each cell reads its own prices and
        allocates its own resources.
        Tier 2 (Intercellular): Cytokines (tissue demand), morphogens
        (positional value), growth factors (investment signal), O₂ tension
        (resource scarcity). These are "market prices" — signals that carry
        tissue-level information between cells. A cell reads its local
        morphogen concentration the way a firm reads the price of steel.
        Tier 3 (Integrator): mTOR — reads all prices simultaneously (amino
        acids, growth factors, energy via AMPK, oxygen via HIF) and makes one
        decision: grow or conserve. This is the entrepreneur reading the full
        local market picture before deciding whether to invest.

    Panel B: "Shadow Prices Shift With Context" — Heatmap of FBA shadow prices
    (dual variables from the iML1515 linear program) for key metabolites across
    four environmental conditions: glucose baseline, acetate carbon switch,
    anaerobic, nitrogen-limited. The same metabolite has completely different
    marginal value depending on the environment the cell is in — NADH is cheap
    on glucose but expensive on acetate; oxygen is nearly free under aerobic
    conditions but becomes the most valuable metabolite under anaerobic. This is
    Menger's subjective value computed from real genome-scale metabolic data.
    The price of a metabolite depends on context, not on the molecule itself.
    Data source: COBRApy FBA on E. coli iML1515 (Monk et al. 2017; 2,712
    reactions, 1,877 metabolites, 1,516 genes).

    Panel C: "Price Discovery in the Distributed Economy" — Metabolite pool
    concentrations over time from the distributed metabolic simulation (13
    pathway agents, shared metabolite pool, no central allocator). The pools
    oscillate early (discovery phase) then converge to stable values
    (equilibrium). When a metabolite overshoots (oversupply), its producers
    slow down — the price drops. When a metabolite gets scarce, producers speed
    up — the price rises. This IS supply-and-demand dynamics happening in a cell.
    No planner needed. The invisible hand finds equilibrium through local
    feedback. The oscillation is the market discovering prices. The stability is
    proof that distributed coordination works.

    Panel D: "Cancer Mutations Target the Price System" — Strip plot showing
    per-cancer-type mutation frequencies for 13 genes that map to the cellular
    price system. Every dot is one cancer type (16 TCGA cancer types shown).
    White line = median. Source: TCGA PanCancerAtlas via cBioPortal (10,967
    samples, 32 cancer types).

    The variation across cancer types IS the signal:
        - TP53 (damage price → apoptosis): 96% in ovarian, 1% in thyroid
        - PIK3CA (PI3K price integrator): 52% in uterine, 3% in ovarian
        - PTEN (mTOR negative regulator): 67% in uterine, 1% in thyroid
        - EGFR (growth factor receptor): 57% in GBM, 0.3% in thyroid
        - STK11 (AMPK energy price reader): 17% in lung adeno, <1% elsewhere

    Different tissues break different price components because different tissues
    rely on different prices. This is tissue-specific misallocation — cancer is
    not one disease but many different failures of the cellular price system.
    The cancer cell is a firm that ignores market prices: it produces what no one
    needs, consumes what everyone needs, and expands regardless of the cost to
    the system.

    Color coding by price system tier:
        Gold = Price Receptors (intercellular signals: EGFR, ERBB2, NOTCH1)
        Blue = Price Integrators (mTOR/AMPK pathway: PIK3CA, PTEN, MTOR, STK11)
        Red = Decision Makers (p53/Rb checkpoint: TP53, RB1, CDKN2A)
        Orange = Spatial Prices (Hippo/contact inhibition: FAT1, NF2)

    The figure's argument in one sentence: living systems have a real price
    system operating at multiple levels, and cancer is specifically the
    destruction of that price system — the calculation problem at the cellular
    level. The disease is the central planning, not the variation.

    ---
    The throughline

    Every single figure answers the same question from a different angle: does biology
    operate like a centrally planned economy or a free market?

    - Layer 1: the network structure says market (no master node, self-regulation,
    feed-forward price signals)
    - Layer 1b: single cells say market (specialization, voluntary exchange, subjective
     value, distributed robustness)
    - Layer 2: metabolic allocation says market (distributed beats centralized under
    perturbation, even against a smart planner with perfect information)
    - Layer 2 FBA: the strongest possible planner (genome-scale LP) still fails on real
     knockout data
    - Layer 2 Price System: the central figure — life has a real price system at
    three levels (metabolite ratios, signaling molecules, mTOR integration), and
    cancer is specifically its destruction. The disease is the central planning,
    not the variation.
    - Layer 3: cross-species gene transfer says market (voluntary exchange succeeds,
    forced exchange fails, trade blocs emerge spontaneously)
