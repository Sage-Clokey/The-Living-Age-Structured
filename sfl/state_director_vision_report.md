## The Problem with the Liberty Movement

The liberty movement has a talent problem. It attracts political operatives and repels engineers. It attracts debaters and repels creators. It attracts people who want to argue about the world and repels the people who could actually build a different one.

This is because the movement frames liberty as a political question — as if the answer to centralized power is a better argument against centralized power. But arguments don't build alternatives. Technology does. Creation does. And the people best equipped to create alternatives — engineers, biologists, designers, builders — leave the movement because they are creators, not politicians. They don't want to fight the machine. They want to grow something that makes the machine irrelevant.

California is full of these people. Bioinformatics students who intuitively understand emergent order but have never connected it to political philosophy. Synthetic biologists who work with living systems every day and already know that centralized control is cancer — literally. Engineers who hold libertarian values but whose only career options seem to be building weapons or serving surveillance infrastructure. Philosophy students who wrestle with conscience and authority but have no framework that connects their questions to the sciences.

My job as State Director is to connect these worlds.

## The Thesis: Life Is the Proof

There are two types of order: centralized and decentralized.

Centralized order requires uniformity. It reduces living things to parts, flattens diversity into compliance, and replaces local knowledge with central planning. It must coerce to maintain itself because nothing alive would choose it. In biology, centralized control has a name: cancer. A cell that overrides the cooperative system and demands all resources flow to itself. In politics, it has many names — but the pattern is the same.

Decentralized order requires diversity. Not random difference, but *ordered diversity* — variation based on local knowledge of time and place that no central planner can predict. Distributed nodes can plan. Central planners cannot. In a distributed system, planning happens in parallel at every node, each making educated decisions based on local knowledge, and the whole is ordered — not in blocks but in spirals. This distributed knowledge is coordinated by prices: the ratio between voluntary exchanges across the network.

This is not a metaphor. This is how life works at every scale:

- **Molecular:** Cells act by choice. They signal other cells, but they respond to those signals voluntarily. There is no master cell, no central command. Proteins interact locally and global function emerges. When cells coerce — when they override the voluntary order — we call them pathogens, viruses, cancer.

- **Cellular:** 37 trillion cells coordinate without a president, a congress, or a plan. The immune system is the defense of the voluntary order — the model of how defense works without a state, from the molecular level all the way up to society. These cells would rather die than be coerced. Apoptosis — programmed cell death — is a cell choosing death over submission to a hijacked program.

- **Ecological:** Forests organize through mycelium networks that share resources underground without central allocation. No forester designed it. Monoculture plantations — centrally planned — collapse. Diverse forests — emergent — endure for millennia.

- **Economic:** Hayek explained that people act based on distributed knowledge from prices, choose what to do, and pivot from one thing to another. This is exactly how biology works: variation directed by distributed knowledge, not random mutation filtered by death.

The fatal conceit — in economics, in biology, in politics — is treating distributed knowing as if it could be centrally held. Treating knowers as if they were merely data.

If DNA is a language, the cell speaks it — and can write in it. Bioinformatics is the study of that distributed knowledge. It is called bio*informatics* because the sequence of DNA holds information, and DNA is a natural language. Life is stories, and bioinformatics is finding those stories so we can learn to become storytellers — not controllers.

**Voluntaryism is written into how biology functions.** This isn't ideology projected onto nature. This is nature revealing the principle that the liberty movement has been arguing about in the wrong language for decades.

## The Shift: From Political Solutions to Creative Ones

> The creation of the Living Age requires media to explain why and technology to show how.

I am not going to run a political organization. I am going to run a creative network that happens to be under the SFL umbrella.

Political organizing pushes away the best people and attracts the worst actors. It turns liberty into a debate team when it should be a design studio. The people who will change the world are not the ones arguing about tax policy — they are the ones designing regenerative agriculture systems, building decentralized biotech, writing software that replaces institutions with protocols, and studying how living systems solve the exact problems that governments fail to solve.

My strategy for California:

### 1. Connect Technology with Philosophy

Host events that are interdisciplinary, not ideological. Not "why government is bad" but "how does a forest organize itself without a manager?" Not pamphlets about the NAP but Socratic conversations that let biology students, engineering students, and philosophy students discover together that they are all studying the same principle from different angles.

I attended SynBioBeta last week and had conversations with synthetic biologists — including Drew Endy, who works with the Hoover Institution — about how to do synthetic biology in ways aligned with voluntaryism. The overlap is natural. These people already think in terms of emergent systems. They just need the philosophical language, and we need their technical language.

### 2. Help Libertarian Engineers Find Work That Creates, Not Destroys

There are engineers across California who hold libertarian values but whose career options funnel them into defense contractors, surveillance companies, or extractive industries. The liberty movement should be helping these people find — or build — work in bioengineering, regenerative systems, open-source tools, and decentralized technology. Not everyone who resonates with liberty wants to go into politics. Most of them want to *make things*. We should be the network that connects them to meaningful work.

### 3. Build a Mycelium Network, Not a Hierarchy

California is too large for centralized control — which is the point. Organize by bioregion: NorCal, Bay Area, Central Valley, SoCal. Each region grows its own way with Local Coordinators who understand local culture. My role is connector and resource provider, not commander.

The network operates like the systems we study:
- Lateral communication between LCs, not just reporting up a chain
- Resources shared across the network, not distributed from the top
- Monthly calls for shared learning, not compliance check-ins
- LCs self-select through resonance, not recruitment quotas

### 4. Use the Socratic Method, Not the Megaphone

The Candles in the Dark approach: don't argue. Ask questions that let people discover the insight themselves.

- *"How does a forest organize itself without a manager?"*
- *"What happens when one cell in your body tries to centralize control?"*
- *"Your body has 37 trillion cells and no government. How?"*

People remember what they discover far longer than what they're told. The person who walks away saying "I've never heard it put that way" is worth more than a hundred sign-ups who forget by Tuesday.

### 5. Document Everything as Content

Every campus conversation, every Socratic event, every connection between biology and philosophy — this is content for the Living Age. The YouTube channel, the writings, the eventually the technology. The SFL role generates the raw material. The Living Age gives it a permanent home. This is not two jobs — it is one spiral.

## The Deeper Frame: Machines vs. Living Things

The problem is the Rockefeller industrial system. The idea that people are machines was institutionalized across education, healthcare, and industry. Treating people as parts in a factory is what fuels the statist mindset. In order to end the state mindset, we must start treating people as living things — and design with life, not against it.

Commander Cody, a clone manufactured in a factory with an inhibitor chip designed to override his will, said it: *"What makes us different from battle droids? We make our own decisions. Our own choices. And we have to live with them too."*

Two parts. The choice *and* the weight. The machine makes no choices — it executes commands. The machine carries no weight — it has no conscience to bear the consequence. The state is the inhibitor chip. My job is to help people remove it, so they can be fully themselves.

The difference between a living thing and a machine is conscience — the connection to what is moral. A living thing chooses and carries what it chose. That weight is not a burden. It is the proof of life.

Eugenics makes the same error as central planning: the fatal conceit that you can design life from the top down. Like the TVA in *Loki* — a fake authority that believes there is one Sacred Timeline, one ideal outcome. But Loki breaks the loom and gives life to the entire multiverse. Because life is many branches, and adaptation isn't about killing what doesn't fit a plan — it is distributed choice based on distributed knowledge.

Evolution is God's proactive creative action. Formed from the dirt, in the image of God, guided by his hand. The diversity of life is not accident — it is architecture. The Creator built systems that grow from the bottom up, not empires that command from the top down.
