The history of how employer-based health insurance became the dominant model through Roosevelt's WWII wage freeze was interesting, but I think there's additional context that helps complete the picture. What the lecture didn't cover is what employer-based insurance actually replaced. Before this system took hold, fraternal societies — mutual aid organizations like lodges and benevolent associations — served as a grassroots model for healthcare. Members pooled resources within their communities to provide affordable care cooperatively. When employer-based insurance became the norm, these fraternal societies were gradually pushed out, replacing a bottom-up, community-centered approach with one dependent on corporate employment.

There's also a broader regulatory story that ties into this shift. Around the same time period, government policies were making it easier for the Rockefeller-influenced pharmaceutical model to dominate the healthcare industry. Through efforts like the Flexner Report, reductionist, pharmaceutical-based medicine became the regulatory standard, while non-reductionist, life-based approaches to health — naturopathy, herbalism, and other holistic practices — were marginalized or stripped of legitimacy. The system didn't just passively evolve; specific policy decisions narrowed what counted as "real" medicine, favoring a model that treats the body as isolated mechanisms to be fixed with drugs rather than a living system to be supported and cultivated. When you put these two shifts together — the loss of fraternal societies and the regulatory entrenchment of reductionist medicine — you get a fuller understanding of why healthcare justice looks the way it does today. It wasn't just market forces; it was deliberate structural choices that shaped who provides care, how it's delivered, and who gets left out.
