# No Human Is Its Sovereign

## I. The Name and the Thing

Mossad is not Jewish. The CIA is not American.

This is not a claim about who staffs them or where their loyalties secretly lie. It is a claim about authority. An institution that takes a people's name is claiming the moral inheritance that comes with it — and an institution that acts against that inheritance has no rightful claim to the name.

Mossad derives its authority from the Israeli state. Not from Sinai. Not from the covenant, the prophets, or the law given to a people who had just been delivered from a king. The CIA derives its authority from statute and executive order — not from the Declaration, not from the proposition that rights are unalienable and precede any government instituted to secure them. Neither agency is accountable to the source it invokes. Each is accountable to the apparatus that funds it.

So the name is borrowed. And once you see that, the natural question is what the name would mean if it were taken seriously. If Israel were more Jewish, and America more American — not in the sense of more zealous, but in the sense of more faithful to the actual content of those inheritances — what would that look like?

It would look like anarchy. Not chaos. Anarchy in its precise sense: *an-arkhos*, no ruler.

## II. The Anarchic Root

The strand is there in all of them, and it is not a marginal reading.

When Israel demands a king, God tells Samuel that the demand is a rejection of God himself. What follows is not a blessing but a warning — the king will take your sons for his chariots, your daughters for his kitchens, a tenth of your grain, a tenth of your flocks, and you will cry out in that day because of the king you have chosen. Conscription and taxation, named as such, presented as the price of wanting a human sovereign.

When the apostles are ordered by the council to stop teaching, the answer is that we must obey God rather than men. Not that men are always wrong, but that when the decree of men and the law of God diverge, the decree has no purchase.

The Islamic confession begins with a negation. *La ilaha illa Allah* — there is no god but God — is a denial of every rival sovereignty before it is an affirmation of anything. Every claimant to ultimate authority is struck down in the first clause.

And the American founding document does not say that government grants rights. It says rights are unalienable, endowed prior to any government, and that government exists to secure what it did not create and cannot revoke.

Four traditions, one structure: there is a law above the ruler, and the ruler is judged by it.

## III. What Anarchy Actually Means

No human rulers.

Not no law — that is the persistent misreading, and it inverts the position entirely. The claim is the opposite of lawlessness. It is that law is real, binding, and not ours to author.

No human can decide what is right and wrong. No legislature can decree that theft is good. No majority can vote murder into justice. Right and wrong exist as part of nature, as part of divine law, and they are discovered through conscience and reason the way the structure of the physical world is discovered. Human rules are legitimate only insofar as they conform to that higher order.

There is law. But no human is its sovereign.

This is what separates the position from legal positivism, which holds that law is simply what the sovereign commands and that an unjust law is still law. Aquinas answered this directly: *lex iniusta non est lex.* An unjust law is not a law but a corruption of one. No decree can make injustice just. A statute that commands theft does not transform theft into something else; it only reveals the legislature as a thief with letterhead.

## IV. Interpretation Is Not Legislation

The obvious objection is that fallible people must still decide particular cases, and deciding looks a great deal like ruling.

It is not the same thing, and the difference is the whole argument.

When a court rules, it is not creating an obligation. It is interpreting one that already exists, and it may get the interpretation wrong. The ruling does not make the thing right — the ruling is an opinion about what was already the case. This is why people can accept a judgment while acknowledging that the judgment may be mistaken, a stance that is simply incoherent if the judgment is what constitutes rightness in the first place. You cannot be wrong about a law you are simultaneously authoring.

The rabbinic tradition makes this explicit in the sharpest possible way. When the sages overrule a heavenly voice, the principle invoked is *lo ba-shamayim hi* — it is not in heaven. The law was given; interpreting it is the work of those who received it. The court's authority is interpretive, not legislative. It does not add to the Torah.

The common law held the same view for centuries. Blackstone's declaratory theory: a judge who decides wrongly has not made bad law, he has failed to find the law, and his error is evidence of what the law is rather than the law itself.

A legislature saying *this is now just because we voted* is making a claim to authorship. A court saying *this is what justice required here* is making a claim to sight. Only one of them is claiming to be sovereign over right and wrong.

## V. Majority Rule Is Fake Democracy

If right is not made by decree, a vote count cannot make it either. Fifty-one percent is a decree with more signatures on it.

A system that binds you to a choice you voted against is not operating on consent in any sense that would survive scrutiny in a contract dispute. Participating in a process is not the same as authorizing its outcome. Spooner's argument stands: a constitution no living person signed binds no one, and the ballot is not a signature.

Consent is individual or it is a fiction. A collective cannot consent on behalf of a person who dissents, because the thing that makes consent morally load-bearing — that this person, with this conscience, agreed to be bound — does not survive aggregation. What survives aggregation is only compulsion with a tally attached.

## VI. Why Adjudication Must Be a Market

This is where the argument arrives, and it follows directly from everything above.

If the arbitrator is not creating law but identifying it, his ruling binds the parties only because they agreed to be bound — not because he rules, and not because he is correct. Consent is the entire source of his authority over them.

Which means the consent has to be real. And consent is only real where there is an alternative. A monopoly arbitrator cannot be consented to in any meaningful sense, because there is nothing to consent to — there is only submission, dressed in the vocabulary of agreement. Where you cannot decline, you have not agreed.

So adjudication must be a market. Multiple forums, chosen by the parties, held to their judgments by reputation rather than by force of monopoly. This is not a hypothetical arrangement. The medieval Law Merchant operated exactly this way: merchant courts with no enforcement arm, whose judgments held because a merchant who defied one lost access to the fairs. Commercial arbitration still runs on chosen forums today.

Choice is what lets the conscience of the individual remain part of the process. A person who selects his arbitrator is exercising judgment about who is likely to see the law rightly — which is a moral act, and the only kind of participation that does not reduce to being counted.

The state's claim is not merely that it will adjudicate. It is that it alone may adjudicate, that you may not choose otherwise, and that your disagreement changes nothing. That is the claim to sovereignty over right and wrong — and it is the one claim that no human institution can legitimately make.

There is law. No human is its sovereign.
