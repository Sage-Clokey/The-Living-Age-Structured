ALPHAGENOME PRESENTATION — SPEAKER SCRIPT
BME 234 — ML and AI in Genomics
=====================================================================

SLIDE 1: TITLE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Today we're presenting AlphaGenome, a paper published in Nature in
January 2026 by Ziga Avsec and colleagues at Google DeepMind.

AlphaGenome is a unified deep learning model for predicting how DNA
sequence determines genome function — and specifically, how genetic
variants affect that function across multiple biological modalities
simultaneously.


SLIDE 2: THE PROBLEM — NON-CODING VARIANTS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
More than 98% of human genetic variation falls in non-coding regions.
These variants don't change protein sequences directly, but they can
alter chromatin accessibility, histone modifications, 3D chromatin
structure, gene expression levels, and splicing patterns.

Experimentally characterizing every non-coding variant is impossible —
there are billions of possible variants. So we need computational
models that can predict variant effects from DNA sequence alone.

The key question this paper asks is: can we build a single model that
captures all of these effects simultaneously, rather than needing
separate models for each one?


SLIDE 3: PRIOR WORK — THE TWO TRADE-OFFS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Before AlphaGenome, models in this space faced two fundamental
trade-offs.

The first is context length versus resolution. Short-context models
like SpliceAI and BPNet take about 10 kilobases of input and predict
at single-base resolution — great for fine detail, but they miss distal
regulatory elements like enhancers that can be hundreds of kilobases
away. Long-context models like Enformer and Borzoi take 200 to 500
kilobases but predict at 128 or 32 base-pair bins — which blurs fine
features like individual splice sites.

AlphaGenome resolves this: it takes 1 megabase of input and predicts
at 1 base-pair resolution.

The second trade-off is between being multimodal versus specialized.
Specialized models like SpliceAI for splicing or ChromBPNet for
chromatin accessibility are very accurate at their one task but can't
capture cross-modal effects. Multimodal models like Enformer and
Borzoi cover more ground but tend to lag behind the specialists.

AlphaGenome unifies both — it's multimodal and matches or beats the
specialists on their own tasks.


SLIDE 4: ALPHAGENOME OVERVIEW
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the high-level picture. AlphaGenome takes 1 megabase of DNA
sequence as input and simultaneously predicts 5,930 human genome
tracks — or 1,128 mouse tracks — across 11 modalities.

These include RNA-seq for gene expression, CAGE and PRO-cap for
transcription initiation, DNase and ATAC for chromatin accessibility,
ChIP-seq for histone modifications and transcription factor binding,
three different splicing outputs, and chromatin contact maps.

Most of these are predicted at single-base-pair resolution. The
histone and TF binding tracks are at 128 bp, and contact maps are at
2,048 bp — matching the resolution of the underlying experimental
assays.


SLIDE 5: MODEL ARCHITECTURE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The architecture is inspired by U-Net, which was originally developed
for biomedical image segmentation. It has three main components.

First, the encoder. This uses convolutional blocks and max pooling to
progressively downsample the input from 1 base-pair resolution down
to 128 base-pair resolution. The convolutional layers are ideal for
detecting local sequence patterns — transcription factor binding
motifs, splice site signals, things that operate over tens of base
pairs.

Second, the transformer tower. This operates at the coarsest 128 bp
resolution and uses self-attention to model long-range dependencies
across the full 1 megabase input. This is where the model learns
things like enhancer-promoter interactions that span hundreds of
kilobases. It also generates 2D pairwise representations that feed
into contact map prediction. The transformer computation is
distributed across 8 TPU devices using sequence parallelism.

Third, the decoder. This upsamples back to 1 bp resolution using skip
connections from corresponding encoder layers. The skip connections
are critical — they preserve the fine-grained local features that
would otherwise be lost through the bottleneck.

The key design insight is that each component does what it's best at:
convolutions handle local patterns, transformers handle long-range
communication, and skip connections bridge the two.


SLIDE 6: TRAINING — PRETRAINING + DISTILLATION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Training happens in two stages.

Stage one is pretraining. They use 4-fold cross-validation on the
reference genome, with data augmentation including random sequence
shifts and reverse complement flipping. The model trains against
observed experimental data — real ChIP-seq, RNA-seq, ATAC-seq tracks.
This produces fold-specific models for evaluation and all-fold teacher
models for the next stage.

Stage two is distillation. A single student model learns to reproduce
the predictions of an ensemble of teacher models. The critical
innovation here is that during distillation, the input sequences are
randomly mutated. This forces the student to learn how sequence
changes affect predictions — essentially training it on millions of
synthetic variants. The result is a single model that's robust for
variant effect scoring, running in under 1 second per variant on an
H100 GPU.

The practical advantage is huge: one distilled model matches the
performance of a 4-model ensemble at one quarter the compute cost.


SLIDE 7: TRACK PREDICTIONS MATCH EXPERIMENTAL DATA
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Before we can trust variant predictions, the model needs to
accurately predict genome tracks on unseen sequences.

This figure shows a full 1-megabase region around chromosome 19 in
HepG2 cells, with observed experimental tracks on top and
AlphaGenome predictions below. You can see strand-specific RNA-seq,
chromatin accessibility from ATAC and DNase, H3K27ac marking active
enhancers, CTCF binding at insulator elements, and Hi-C contact maps
showing 3D chromatin interactions. The predictions match closely
across all modalities.

The zoomed view of the LDLR gene shows the real advantage of 1 bp
resolution: you can see individual splice donor and acceptor sites,
splice site usage predictions, and splice junction arcs with read
counts. This level of detail is simply impossible at 32 or 128 bp
resolution.


SLIDE 8: QUANTITATIVE TRACK PERFORMANCE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Quantitatively, AlphaGenome achieves strong Pearson correlations
between predicted and observed tracks across all modalities. Splice
site usage hits r = 0.86, RNA-seq r = 0.81, ATAC r = 0.73.

Raw gene expression prediction is strong at r = 0.82, but capturing
cell-type-specific expression differences — which genes are higher
in liver versus brain — remains more challenging, at r = 0.59 to
0.63. The authors acknowledge this as an ongoing frontier.


SLIDE 9: SPLICING — A COMPREHENSIVE VIEW
---------------------------------------------------------------------
AlphaGenome is the only model that predicts all four aspects of
splicing at 1 bp resolution: RNA-seq coverage, splice site
classification, splice site usage, and splice junction counts.

The DLG1 example is compelling. A 4-base-pair deletion in a patient
causes exon skipping in tibial artery tissue. AlphaGenome correctly
predicts the loss of the splice junction flanking the skipped exon,
the emergence of a new bypass junction that skips over it, and the
drop in RNA-seq coverage over that exon. It captures the full
biological consequence from sequence alone.

The COL6A2 example shows the model can also predict gain of function:
a G-to-C variant creates a new splice donor, and the model predicts
the resulting new junction and extended exon.


SLIDE 10: THE MODEL LEARNS SPLICING BIOLOGY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
One of the most interesting results is what the model has learned
about splicing biology. Using in silico mutagenesis — systematically
mutating every position around a splice site and measuring the effect
— the authors show that AlphaGenome independently discovered all the
canonical splicing signals: the branch point adenine, the
polypyrimidine tract, the AG acceptor dinucleotide, exonic splicing
enhancers, and the GT donor dinucleotide.

Nobody told the model these signals exist. It learned them entirely
from data. And on splicing QTL benchmarks, it beats all specialized
splicing models including Pangolin, SpliceAI, and DeltaSplice.


SLIDE 11: GENE EXPRESSION VARIANT EFFECTS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
For expression variants, the scoring strategy is straightforward:
predict RNA-seq coverage for the reference and alternate alleles,
average over the target gene's exons, and take the log difference.

On 17,675 eQTLs from GTEx, this gives a Spearman correlation of 0.50
with observed effect sizes. But the practical payoff is the real
story: at a 90% sign accuracy threshold — meaning you want to be 90%
sure you're getting the direction of the effect right — AlphaGenome
recovers 41% of eQTLs. Borzoi recovers only 19%. That's more than
double the actionable predictions from a single model.


SLIDE 12: CHROMATIN ACCESSIBILITY & TF BINDING
---------------------------------------------------------------------
For chromatin accessibility variants, AlphaGenome achieves r = 0.74
correlation with observed caQTL effect sizes across 2,219 causal
variants. That's very strong quantitative agreement.

But what's really powerful is the mechanistic interpretation. When a
G-to-T variant reduces DNase accessibility, in silico mutagenesis
reveals that the variant disrupts an NF-kB binding motif. The model
doesn't just tell you that accessibility decreases — it tells you
why. It identifies the transcription factor whose binding site was
disrupted. Same for SPI1 binding QTLs, where ISM reveals which SPI1
motifs are created or destroyed by variants.


SLIDE 13: TAL1 ONCOGENE — THE MULTIMODAL CASE STUDY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the most important figure in the paper, because it
demonstrates why a unified multimodal model matters.

Three independent groups of non-coding mutations have been found in
T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia patients, all of which upregulate
the TAL1 oncogene. The authors tested one of these — a C-to-ACG
insertion — and from a single inference pass, AlphaGenome predicts
the full mechanistic cascade: increased H3K27ac and H3K4me1 at the
variant site, indicating neo-enhancer formation; decreased H3K9me3
and H3K27me3 near the transcription start site, indicating loss of
repressive marks; increased H3K36me3 across the gene body, indicating
active transcription; and increased TAL1 mRNA expression.

In silico mutagenesis reveals that the insertion creates a MYB
transcription factor binding motif — which is exactly what was
discovered experimentally by Mansour and colleagues in 2014.

No single-modality model could have captured this story. You need
histone marks, chromatin state, TF binding, and expression all
together to see the full mechanism. This is the strongest argument
for the unified approach.


SLIDE 14: ABLATIONS — WHAT DESIGN CHOICES MATTER?
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The authors ran extensive ablation studies to understand which design
choices contribute to performance.

First, resolution: 1 bp training consistently beats coarser
resolutions, especially for splicing and accessibility tasks.

Second, sequence length: 1 Mb training with 1 Mb inference is
optimal. Models trained on short sequences perform worse even when
given long sequences at inference time — the model genuinely learns
to use long-range regulatory information during training.

Third, distillation: a single distilled student from 64 teachers
rivals a 4-model ensemble, at a quarter of the compute.

Fourth, multimodal training: the full multimodal model generally
outperforms single-modality models. Expression and accessibility data
provide the most benefit to other tasks through cross-modal transfer.


SLIDE 15: LIMITATIONS & FUTURE DIRECTIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
The authors are transparent about limitations.

Distal regulatory elements beyond 100 kilobases are still challenging
— performance decays with distance. Tissue-specific patterns need
improvement. The model is only trained on human and mouse. It focuses
on protein-coding genes, underserving non-coding RNAs like miRNAs.
It predicts molecular consequences, not phenotype — you can't go
directly from "this variant reduces expression" to "this variant
causes disease." And it hasn't been benchmarked on personal genome
prediction, which is a known weakness of models in this space.

Future directions include more species, single-cell data integration,
new modalities like DNA methylation, integration with conservation
scores for clinical use, fine-tuning on perturbational datasets, and
uncertainty estimation.


SLIDE 16: SUMMARY
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Six key takeaways.

One: AlphaGenome unifies long-range context, base-pair resolution,
and multimodal prediction into a single model.

Two: The U-Net plus Transformer architecture, enabled by sequence
parallelism across 8 TPUs, is what makes this tractable.

Three: The two-stage pretraining plus distillation pipeline produces
a single robust model for variant scoring.

Four: It achieves state of the art on 25 of 26 variant effect tasks
and 22 of 24 track prediction tasks.

Five: The TAL1 case study proves that multimodal scoring reveals
mechanisms invisible to single-modality approaches.

Six: The practical impact is real — 2x more eQTLs at 90% accuracy,
under 1 second per variant, and publicly available through an API and
GitHub.


SLIDE 18: BEYOND VARIANTS — THE DISTRIBUTED KNOWLEDGE PERSPECTIVE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Now we want to step back and offer a broader critique of AlphaGenome's
approach — drawing on ideas from Natural Genetic Engineering and
Austrian economics.

AlphaGenome scores individual variants against a single reference
genome. But there are fundamental limitations to this framing.

First, variants are not the unit of adaptation. Cells don't evolve
through isolated point mutations. They adapt through coordinated
operations — what James Shapiro calls Natural Genetic Engineering:
domain shuffling, transposon deployment, regulatory element
mobilization, horizontal gene transfer. These are multi-locus,
structured events. Scoring one variant at a time is like evaluating
an economy one transaction at a time — you see the price change but
miss the market logic.

Second, the reference genome functions as a central plan. Everything
is compared to one consensus sequence that doesn't represent any
actual person. Hayek's knowledge problem tells us that no single
snapshot can contain the distributed knowledge of a complex system.
The real knowledge is in population diversity — the 150,000 genomes
in UK Biobank, the haplotype variation across populations. By
treating the reference as ground truth, variation gets framed as
deviation rather than as distributed knowledge.

Third, context is flattened. The model uses tissue labels as fixed
categories, but Hayek's core insight is that knowledge is local —
the agent on the ground knows things the central planner can't. A
variant in a liver cell under metabolic stress is a fundamentally
different event than the same variant in a resting neuron. The cell
as an active agent determines what a variant means, and AlphaGenome
has no model of that agency.


SLIDE 19: THE GENOME AS READ-WRITE LEDGER
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Three more limitations follow from thinking about the genome as a
distributed, read-write system.

Fourth, AlphaGenome treats the genome as passive and read-only —
sequence in, prediction out. But Shapiro's Natural Genetic
Engineering framework shows the genome is read-write. The cell
actively modifies its own DNA in response to conditions: stress-
directed mutagenesis, transposon activation, chromatin remodeling.
When you introduce a variant, the cell doesn't just passively accept
it. It may compensate, silence, amplify, or repair. AlphaGenome
predicts the first-order effect but not the second-order cellular
response.

Fifth, there is no model of distributed validation. In a blockchain,
a transaction isn't final until the network validates it. In biology,
a genetic change isn't final until the cell's regulatory network
integrates it — or rejects it. DNA repair, nonsense-mediated decay,
chromatin silencing, immune surveillance — these are the cell's
consensus mechanisms. AlphaGenome predicts what a variant would do
if the cell passively accepted it, not whether the cell would
actually let it happen. This is exactly why synthetic biology
constructs fail — they pass the sequence-level check but get
rejected by the cell's own validation system.

Sixth, and most fundamentally: this is what Hayek called the fatal
conceit — the belief that a central authority can possess enough
knowledge to predict a distributed system. AlphaGenome is a central
planner with very good data. It gets the broad strokes right. But it
will always hit a ceiling, because the relevant knowledge isn't only
in the sequence — it's distributed across the network of cells,
tissues, and organisms that read that sequence, and that knowledge
is contextual, dynamic, and cannot be fully centralized into a
single model.


SLIDE 19: DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
We have four questions for the class.

First: AlphaGenome is trained on reference genomes. How might it
perform on variants from underrepresented populations whose haplotype
backgrounds differ significantly from the reference?

Second: The model predicts molecular consequences but not phenotype.
What additional information or models would be needed to go from
"this variant reduces expression by 30%" to "this variant increases
disease risk"?

Third: The distillation step uses random mutations to improve variant
robustness. Could this introduce biases — for example, could the
model learn to over-predict effects near certain sequence contexts
like CpG sites?

Fourth: How does AlphaGenome relate to ChromHMM, which we discussed
earlier in the course? ChromHMM discovers chromatin states from
observed experimental data; AlphaGenome predicts chromatin states
from sequence alone. What are the complementary strengths of each
approach?
