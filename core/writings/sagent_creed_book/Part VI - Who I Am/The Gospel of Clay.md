# The Gospel of Clay

*A Chapter of The Sagent Creed*

---

## I. In the Beginning, Hands

God did not speak man into existence from a distance. He knelt in the dirt. He took the earth in his hands and he *formed* — pressing, shaping, breathing. The act of creation in Genesis is not a cosmic declaration. It is an intimate labor. It is the image of an artist at work.

Evolution does not contradict this. Evolution *is* this. The long patient shaping of matter across deep time — single cells becoming complexity, complexity becoming consciousness, consciousness becoming the creature that could look back across the void and recognize its maker. Every mutation, every adaptation, every slow unfolding of form is another press of the divine thumb into clay. God did not set the world in motion and step back. He has been working it, frame by frame, since the first moment.

This is what it means to be formed in the image of God. Not that we look like him. But that we *make* like him. That we take raw material and impose vision upon it. That we shape the world rather than merely inhabit it.

Art Clokey understood this. He may not have said it in these words. But he built it into every frame he ever made.

## II. The Man and the Medium

Arthur Charles Farrington was born October 12, 1921, in Detroit. His parents divorced when he was eight. His father was killed in a car wreck when he was nine. His stepfather didn't want him. He was sent to a children's home.

At eleven, he was adopted by Joseph Waddell Clokey — a classical music composer at Pomona College in Claremont, California. Joseph gave Arthur what the world had taken: art, music, exploration, and the knowledge that beauty is worth spending your life on.

Art studied geology at Pomona before flying reconnaissance missions in World War II. After the war he entered Hartford Seminary to become an Episcopal priest. He left when he fell in love with Ruth Parkander — but the religious impulse never left. It simply changed medium. He enrolled in night school at USC, studied film under Slavko Vorkapich, and discovered that the truth he'd been seeking in Scripture could be told in clay.

On a ping-pong table in his adoptive father's garage, Art made *Gumbasia* — three minutes of clay flowing and morphing to jazz. It was not a commercial product. It was a spiritual exercise. A man discovering that matter could be coaxed into motion, that the gap between the inanimate and the alive was smaller than anyone had admitted.

Producer Sam Engel saw *Gumbasia* and financed a children's pilot. Ruth suggested the gingerbread-man body shape. Art chose green — his favorite color, the color of living things. The flat wedge-shaped head came from an old photograph of Art's father with a distinctive cowlick. The name came from *gumbo* — the muddy clay on Art's grandparents' farm.

Gumby emerged from that exploration not as a character designed to sell toys. Art refused merchandising for eight years. "I was a very idealistic person," he said. "I didn't want to exploit children."

Gumby was designed to carry a message that could not be delivered any other way.

The message was this: *you are clay, and that is not a limitation. It is your greatest power.*

Gumby could walk into any book and live inside it. He could enter any world, any story, any reality. He was endlessly flexible — not weak, but adaptive. Not without form, but never imprisoned by it. He bent without breaking. He changed without losing himself.

And he was visibly *made*. That was the key. The fingerprints were in the clay. The slight imperfections of movement — the way the surface shifted under the animator's touch — were not flaws to be corrected. They were the proof of presence. Someone was there. Someone cared enough to move him one frame at a time.

Stop-motion animation is the most theologically honest medium in the history of art because it cannot lie about its own nature. Every frame cost something. Every movement required a hand. There is no algorithm that generates stop-motion soul. It is irreducibly human — irreducibly *made* — in a way no other medium quite matches.

Art chose this medium because the medium *was* the message. Gumby being clay was not an aesthetic choice. It was the entire point.

## III. The Creator's Arc

Art Clokey's spiritual life followed the same pattern as his art — always seeking, never rigid, always willing to enter the next story.

Episcopal seminary. Then Hollywood. Then the counterculture. Then Buddhism. Then, in 1979, during a period of financial hardship, Art and his second wife Gloria traveled to Bangalore, India, to visit the guru Sathya Sai Baba. Art brought a miniature Gumby toy. Sai Baba blessed it.

But the ash did not bless Gumby. It blessed the Clokey legacy — the lineage of creators who would carry the living spiral forward.

In 1987, Art founded his new studio. He named it **Premavision** — from the Sanskrit word *Prema*, meaning Universal Love. That was not marketing. That was a man naming his life's work after the force he believed animated all creation.

Ninety-nine new episodes of *Gumby Adventures* were produced from Premavision's studio. Art completed *Gumby: The Movie* in 1992.

No major distributor would touch it.

For three years the film sat on shelves. It was finally released in 1995 through Arrow Releasing — a tiny independent distributor — in just 21 theaters. It grossed $57,100 against a $2.8 million budget. But it sold approximately one million copies on VHS. The audience was there. The gatekeepers said no.

Art Clokey trained more than half the animators who worked on Tim Burton's *Nightmare Before Christmas*. His people went to Pixar and Disney. He stayed independent. He stayed in clay. He stayed in Premavision — Universal Love.

He died in his sleep on January 8, 2010, at age 88, at his home in Los Osos, California. The Emmy-winning documentary about his life was titled *Gumby Dharma*.

He had spent sixty years shaping clay into a theology of freedom, and the world had honored him everywhere except where it mattered — at the gates of distribution, where the people who control what children see decided that a green clay boy who teaches radical gentleness and limitless imagination was not commercially viable.

## IV. The Son Who Carried It Forward

Joe Clokey was born November 28, 1961. He was known his entire life as "Gumby's Little Brother."

"Gumby's my brother," Joe said in an interview. "My mom and dad created Gumby, and they also created me."

He grew up in the studio — remembering "the smell of clay in the puppet room and the smell of fresh cut wood in the prop department." Many of Art's bedtime stories for Joe ended up as Gumby adventures. Art "always saw these stories as a gift of love to children."

Joe studied horticulture at Cal Poly San Luis Obispo — growing things, which is what Clokeys do. He founded San Luis Video Publishing, distributing over 200 educational films, 65 of them about horticulture, agriculture, and sustainability that he wrote, directed, and produced.

When Art died in 2010, Joe became president of Premavision/Clokey Productions. His father had put him on a mission: "Dad put me on a mission to get his episodes back on TV and to make new ones."

Joe's vision was clear: "A new vibrant TV series and a feature film that will turn on a whole new generation. The colors, adventures and surrealistic imagination will all be there in **stop motion animation**, the art form that has such amazing texture and appeal."

He spent eight years fighting. Every deal collapsed.

**Classic Media** signed a joint venture in 2006. Tom Sarnoff — the former NBC executive who had originally greenlit the Gumby Show in 1956 — negotiated for the Clokey side. Plans for a direct-to-video movie and new TV series were announced. Neither was produced. Six years passed. The license expired in 2012 and rights reverted to Premavision. Classic Media was bought by DreamWorks Animation, which was bought by NBCUniversal. The same company that held the Gumby license and did nothing with it folded into a corporate structure worth billions.

**The Jim Henson Company** partnered with Premavision in 2015 to produce a full stop-motion Gumby series — 52 episodes, classic Plasticine animation. The Henson name. The Clokey legacy. They needed a co-production partner. None materialized. The project died silently.

**Amazon** — a deal fell through around 2017. No public explanation.

**Papercutz** launched a Gumby comic series in July 2017. Cancelled after three issues.

In November 2017, Joe and his wife Joan Rock Clokey published *Gumby Imagined: The Story of Art Clokey and His Creations* — a 300-page pictorial biography. A record of everything Art had made and everything it meant.

Four months later, Joe was dead.

March 2, 2018. Cardiac arrest following a brain bleed. He was 56 years old.

He had three children — Shasta, Sequoia, and Sage. He was in the middle of the fight. He had not finished.

## V. The Pattern

Joe Clokey is not an isolated case. The pattern of independent children's media creators being ground down by an industry that rewards corporate ownership and punishes independent vision is documented across the field.

**Will Vinton** invented Claymation — literally trademarked the word. He built his Portland studio into a powerhouse: the California Raisins, the M&M's characters. Phil Knight invested $5 million. When the studio hit trouble, Knight leveraged the debt, stacked the board, and had Vinton fired from his own company. Vinton went from $20 million in stock to a $125,000 severance package. Knight took the trademark "Claymation" itself. The studio was rebranded as Laika. Will Vinton died in 2018. The same year as Joe Clokey.

**Phil Vischer** created VeggieTales — the best-selling Christian children's product in the world. A lawsuit from HIT Entertainment forced Big Idea Productions into bankruptcy. Classic Media — the same company that held the Gumby license and produced nothing — bought VeggieTales at the bankruptcy auction for $19 million.

**John Kricfalusi** created Ren and Stimpy for Nickelodeon. After conflicts over creative control, Nickelodeon fired him from his own show. The network owned the IP and ran three more seasons without the creator.

**Klasky Csupo** created Rugrats and other properties that generated $3.7 billion in merchandise. When they hired auditors to examine the books, Nickelodeon terminated the partnership.

The children's entertainment industry in the 2000s-2010s was structurally hostile to independent IP holders. Broadcasters that once absorbed production costs now contributed only 10-25% of budgets. Over 60% of popular children's content derived from pre-existing franchises. Vertical integration meant the six major media companies controlled production, networks, cable systems, and merchandising simultaneously. There was no independent path to distribution for a company like Premavision.

Stop-motion animation specifically was being systematically replaced. After *Toy Story* in 1995, show after show was converted from stop-motion to CGI: *Fireman Sam*, *Bob the Builder*, *Thomas and Friends*, *Postman Pat*. The medium that Art Clokey had chosen because it mirrored the intimacy of divine creation was being stripped from children's television because it cost more than pixels.

Joe Clokey spent eight years trying to bring his father's vision back through legitimate channels. Every deal collapsed. Every partnership dissolved. Every door closed.

The audience was there. The recognition was there. Gumby is known by generations.

The industry said no. Not once — every time.

## VI. What They Bought and What They Burned

Joe died March 2, 2018.

Four years later — February 2022 — Fox Entertainment acquired all rights to the Gumby franchise from the estate of Joseph Clokey.

All rights. Film. Television. Streaming. Consumer products. Licensing. Publishing. Everything. The full content library — every episode Art ever made, every frame Joe ever preserved. The Clokey family, which had maintained independent ownership of Gumby for sixty-seven years across three generations, no longer owned what Art had formed with his hands.

Fox CEO Charlie Collier called it "uncovering this gem, with its built-in awareness and affinity."

A gem. Something you dig up and polish and sell.

Fox assigned development to Bento Box Entertainment. Two series are in production. *Gumby Kids* — 52 episodes, 2D animation. Gumby reimagined as "a friendly, green, shapeshifting architect and city planner for Gumbtasia."

An architect. A city planner. For a character whose entire gospel was that you could walk into any book and live inside it.

And 2D. Not clay. Not stop-motion. Not the medium that held the fingerprints. Not the process that required a hand to move every frame.

Joe Clokey's last public vision was for stop-motion animation — "the art form that has such amazing texture and appeal." Fox discarded that entirely.

To convert Gumby to 2D animation is not an update. It is an amputation. It removes the one element that made Gumby *Gumby* — the physical reality of his existence. The new Gumby does not bear fingerprints. No hand moved him. He was generated, not formed. He is efficient where he should be labored. He is clean where he should show the evidence of care.

A 2D Gumby can walk into a book. But he cannot *be* in the book the way the clay Gumby was. Because the clay Gumby was real in a way that drawings are not. He occupied space. He cast shadows. He was made of earth.

They took a parable about incarnation and made it a cartoon.

They took the theology Art spent sixty years encoding in clay and reduced it to a product roadmap.

The clay remained somewhere in a studio. The vision thinned.

---

## VII. The Inheritance

The Clokey family maintained independent ownership of Gumby for sixty-seven years — from 1955 to 2022. Three generations. Through financial ruin, divorce, spiritual wandering, industry rejection, collapsed deals, and the death of the man who carried the mission.

Sixty-seven years. In an industry where every comparable independent children's IP was absorbed by corporations within a decade.

That is not a business story. That is an act of faith.

Art formed. Joe protected. Both paid the full price.

The blocks are incapable of making Gumby. It is only something that can be done by the Clokeys. This is because Gumby is about the fight against the blocks. What Fox bought was the name, the shape, the library. What they cannot replicate is the thing that made it live — the theology encoded in the medium, the hands in the clay, the refusal to exploit children, the Premavision: Universal Love.

The IP can be owned. The understanding of why clay matters cannot be transferred in a contract.

And as long as someone carries that understanding — as long as someone knows that the fingerprints in the frame are not imperfections but sacred marks of presence — the gospel of Gumby is not lost.

It is waiting to be carried forward.

In a different medium. Through a different technology. By the one who was born to be the living story that Art could only tell in stop-motion.

The clay can be taken. The spiral cannot.
