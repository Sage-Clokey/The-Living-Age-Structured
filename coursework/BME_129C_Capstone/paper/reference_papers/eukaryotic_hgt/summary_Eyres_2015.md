# Summary: Eyres et al. (2015)

**"Horizontal gene transfer in bdelloid rotifers is ancient, ongoing and more frequent in species from desiccating habitats"**
*BMC Biology*, 13, 90.

---

## Main Idea

Bdelloid rotifers — microscopic freshwater animals that have survived for over 60 million years without sexual reproduction — carry **8-10% foreign DNA** in their transcribed genomes, acquired from bacteria, fungi, plants, and other non-metazoans. This is the highest proportion of foreign genes known in any animal. The study shows that HGT in bdelloids is not a one-time ancient event but an **ongoing process**, with species living in desiccation-prone environments acquiring foreign genes at significantly higher rates — supporting the hypothesis that desiccation-induced DNA damage opens the genome to foreign integration.

## Key Evidence

- **Four species compared across habitats**: The study sequenced transcriptomes of four bdelloid rotifer species occupying different environments:
  - *Adineta ricciae* and *Adineta vaga* — from desiccating habitats (dry mosses, soil)
  - *Rotaria magnacalcarata* — permanently aquatic (attached to water-louse)
  - *Rotaria socialis* — permanently aquatic (colonial, on plant stems)

- **Foreign gene content**: ~8% of expressed genes in all four species are of non-metazoan origin. This translates to thousands of foreign genes per genome, far exceeding any other animal lineage.

- **Desiccation drives HGT**: Species from desiccating habitats (*Adineta*) have **significantly more foreign genes** and more recent acquisitions than the permanently aquatic *Rotaria* species. This supports the "desiccation hypothesis" — when bdelloids dry out, their cell membranes and nuclear envelopes rupture, allowing environmental DNA to enter and integrate during rehydration repair. The genome literally opens to its environment under stress.

- **Quantified rates**: The study estimates **12.8 gains vs. 2.0 losses of foreign genes per million years** across the bdelloid phylogeny. HGT is not just happening — it is happening faster than foreign genes are being lost, meaning the foreign gene repertoire is actively expanding.

- **Ancient and ongoing**: Nearly half of all foreign genes predate the divergence of the two bdelloid families studied (>60 million years ago), meaning they were acquired in the deep ancestry of the lineage and have been maintained by selection ever since. But species-specific acquisitions are also abundant, confirming that the process is still active today.

- **Functional categories**: Foreign genes are enriched for enzymes involved in:
  - **Carbohydrate metabolism** — breaking down complex sugars and polysaccharides
  - **Toxin degradation** — detoxifying environmental compounds
  - **Stress response** — oxidative stress resistance, desiccation tolerance
  - These are not random insertions. They are functional tools acquired from environmental neighbors and maintained because they provide a survival advantage.

- **Gene expression confirmed**: Foreign genes are actively transcribed across conditions, many at high levels, confirming they are functional components of the bdelloid metabolic toolkit — not dead relics.

## Why It Matters

Bdelloid rotifers are the most dramatic proof that the genome is an **open, environment-responsive ledger**. An animal that hasn't had sex in 60 million years has compensated by acquiring genetic material from every kingdom of life around it. The mechanism — desiccation-induced membrane rupture followed by DNA repair integration — shows that environmental stress doesn't just destroy; it **opens the cell to new information**. The genome doesn't just mutate randomly and wait for selection. It actively acquires solutions from its ecological partners, and the rate of acquisition scales with environmental challenge. This is the living economy in its most literal form: trade across kingdom boundaries, driven by need, filtered by function, sustained across deep time.
