# Summary: Husnik & McCutcheon (2018)

**"Functional horizontal gene transfer from bacteria to eukaryotes"**
*Nature Reviews Microbiology*, 16, 67-79.

---

## Main Idea

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from bacteria to eukaryotes is not rare or irrelevant — it is widespread, functionally significant, and follows predictable patterns. The authors propose a **"weak-link model"** explaining how bacterial DNA enters eukaryotic genomes: endosymbiotic bacteria living inside eukaryotic cells eventually lyse, releasing DNA directly into the host cytoplasm near the nucleus. This proximity creates a recurring opportunity for integration, especially during DNA repair when the nuclear envelope is permeable.

## Key Evidence

- **The weak-link model**: Endosymbiont lysis releases DNA into the host cell. During double-strand break repair, foreign DNA can be captured and integrated into the nuclear genome via non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). This explains why organisms with intracellular symbionts (insects, protists, nematodes) show the highest rates of bacteria-to-eukaryote HGT.

- **Two categories of functional HGT**:
  1. **Maintenance HGTs** — compensate for gene losses in degenerating endosymbiont genomes. Example: the mealybug *Planococcus citri* has replaced lost symbiont genes with bacterial genes transferred to its own nucleus, keeping the metabolic partnership alive even as the symbiont genome decays.
  2. **Innovation HGTs** — provide entirely new capabilities the host lineage never had. Examples include:
     - **Herbivory enzymes**: Plant cell wall-degrading enzymes (cellulases, pectinases) transferred from bacteria to nematodes, beetles, and stick insects, enabling them to digest plant material independently.
     - **Antibacterial weapons**: Lysozyme-like genes in *Acyrthosiphon pisum* (pea aphid) acquired from bacteria, used to defend against bacterial pathogens.
     - **Extreme environment survival**: Tardigrades and bdelloid rotifers carry bacterial genes for desiccation tolerance and oxidative stress resistance.

- **Scale**: Hundreds of confirmed functional HGTs across diverse eukaryotic lineages — insects, nematodes, protists, plants, and even some vertebrates. These are not pseudogenes or artifacts; many show active transcription, proper splicing with acquired introns, and measurable phenotypic effects.

- **Stringent criteria**: The authors emphasize that confirming functional HGT requires (1) phylogenetic evidence of bacterial origin, (2) evidence of nuclear integration (introns, eukaryotic promoters), (3) expression data, and (4) ideally, functional validation. Many candidates pass all four tests.

## Why It Matters

This paper dismantles the textbook claim that HGT is "a prokaryotic phenomenon." Eukaryotic genomes are not closed books written only by vertical inheritance and random mutation. They are open ledgers that actively acquire, integrate, and use foreign genetic material — especially from intimate bacterial partners. The genome is a read-write system shaped by ecological relationships, not a static blueprint passively accumulating errors.
