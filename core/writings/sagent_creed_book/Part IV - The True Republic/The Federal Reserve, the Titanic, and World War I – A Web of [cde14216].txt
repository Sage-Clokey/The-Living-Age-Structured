The Federal Reserve, the Titanic, and World War I – A Web of Power and Deception

At first glance, the Federal Reserve, the sinking of the Titanic, and the outbreak of World War I seem unrelated.
But look deeper, and you will find a trail of manipulation, power struggles, and financial control.

The years leading up to 1913 saw a battle for the future of money, war, and global influence.
The sinking of the Titanic, the creation of the Federal Reserve, and the outbreak of WWI were not isolated events—they were part of a larger plan.


---

1. The Federal Reserve – The Coup Against Economic Freedom

Before 1913, the U.S. had no central bank.
Money was backed by gold, and private banks operated independently.

But powerful elites—J.P. Morgan, Paul Warburg, and the banking dynasties of Europe—wanted total control.
They knew that a central bank would allow them to print money, create debt, and dominate the economy.

In 1910, at Jekyll Island, a secret meeting took place between the most powerful bankers in the world.

Their goal: Create a central banking system that would be controlled by private interests but disguised as a government institution.

In 1913, the Federal Reserve Act was passed, giving a private cartel control over the U.S. money supply.


But there was a problem: powerful men opposed it.


---

2. The Titanic – The Convenient Disaster

Some of the most powerful opponents of the Federal Reserve were wealthy businessmen who believed in gold-backed money and decentralized banking.

Among them were:

John Jacob Astor IV – One of the wealthiest men in the world, opposed to a central bank.

Benjamin Guggenheim – Industrialist, against fiat money.

Isidor Straus – Co-owner of Macy’s, strong critic of the banking elite.


All three were aboard the Titanic.
All three died when it sank on April 15, 1912.

Meanwhile, J.P. Morgan—one of the architects of the Federal Reserve—had a last-minute cancellation and did not board the ship.

Coincidence? Or was the Titanic a way to eliminate opposition?

Regardless of the intent, the result was clear: With key opponents gone, the Federal Reserve Act passed in 1913.

3. World War I – The First Test of the Federal Reserve

Just months after the Federal Reserve was created, World War I began.

Wars require massive funding.

Central banks profit from war by printing money and lending to governments.

The Federal Reserve allowed the U.S. to enter WWI without worrying about gold-backed money.


With a central bank in place, the U.S. could finance war through debt instead of taxation.
The government borrowed billions, enriching the banks and plunging the country into perpetual debt.

World War I was the first great test of the Federal Reserve—and it proved that war and banking were now inseparable.
