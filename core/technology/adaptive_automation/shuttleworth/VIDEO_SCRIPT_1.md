# Video 1: Who Are You and What Do You Want to Change?

**Target length:** 2–3 minutes
**Tone:** Direct, personal, unhurried. Not a pitch. A conversation.

---

## Script

Everything we build is dead.

The walls of every house, the frame of every building, the material of every city —
dead. Extracted, processed, assembled, fixed. It doesn't respond when you press on
it. It doesn't repair itself when it cracks. It doesn't know what season it is.
It was never alive, and it will never be alive, and we have accepted this as though
it were a law of nature.

It isn't.

I'm a bioinformatics student at UC Santa Cruz. I work with genomes for a living —
reading the code that makes organisms what they are, understanding how a single
strand of DNA can contain the instructions to grow a tree, a mycelium network,
a coral reef. And the longer I spend inside that code, the more convinced I am
that we are standing at the beginning of something that has no historical precedent.

We are learning to speak the language of life.

Not metaphorically. Literally. We now have protein language models trained on
hundreds of millions of sequences that can generate functional proteins the way
a language model generates sentences. We have regulatory models that predict
how DNA controls when and where genes turn on. We are beginning — just beginning —
to understand the gene regulatory networks that cause a single fertilized cell
to build a complex three-dimensional organism.

The question I am trying to answer is: what happens when this knowledge becomes
a design tool?

What I want to change is this: I want the next generation of buildings to be alive.

Not metaphorically alive. Structurally alive. Grown from organisms engineered
to be load-bearing, self-repairing, responsive to their environment, shaped by
their conditions rather than imposed on them. A wall that heals when it cracks.
A structure that hardens over years rather than degrading. A building that is
part of the ecosystem it sits in rather than a wound in it.

This is not science fiction. The organisms that make this possible — mycelium,
bacterial cellulose, coral-derived biomineralization pathways, silk proteins,
plant morphogen gradients — they already exist. The biology is real. What doesn't
exist yet is the design software. The interface between human intention and
biological possibility.

That's what I'm building. A system that lets you describe what you want in plain
English and receive back a designed organism — protein sequences, regulatory circuits,
developmental programs — codon-optimized for the chassis that will grow it.

And I'm building it open. Because the grammar of life should not be owned.

I believe we are at the beginning of the Living Age — the point in history where
life becomes the primary material of human civilization. That transition will either
happen in the hands of a few large corporations with proprietary tools and
extraction-based models, or it will happen as open infrastructure that anyone
can build on.

I am here to make the case for the second option.
And to build it.

---

## Delivery Notes

- Speak slowly at the opening — "Everything we build is dead" needs space to land
- The "we are learning to speak the language of life" line is the turn — let it breathe
- The list of organisms (mycelium, bacterial cellulose, coral...) should feel like
  evidence, not enthusiasm — factual, grounded
- "The grammar of life should not be owned" is a complete thought — pause after it
- End calm, not pitched up — this is a statement of intent, not a sales close
